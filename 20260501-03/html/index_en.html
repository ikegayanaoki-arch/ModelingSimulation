Key Points

1. Review of the one-dimensional steady heat conduction equation

Discretization using the Finite Volume Method (FVM)

Review of the procedure for discretizing the one-dimensional steady heat conduction equation studied last week using the Finite Volume Method (FVM).

The target domain is divided into “nodes,” and each node is located at the center of a volume and represents the temperature at that point.

The governing equation is integrated over a control volume, and Gauss’s theorem is used to convert the second-derivative term into first-derivative terms evaluated at the boundaries, East (E) and West (W).

The temperature gradient at the boundary is approximated by the ratio of the temperature difference between adjacent nodes to the distance between them.

By substituting these expressions into the integrated equation and rearranging, a discretized equation is derived to compute the temperature Ti at node i from neighboring nodes Ti+1 and Ti-1.

The equation is solved by an iterative method, starting from predicted values (random values or values from the previous step) and repeating until convergence.

Three simulation cases and results

Three one-dimensional heat-conduction cases were considered.

Case 1: Perfect insulation

An idealized situation with a linear temperature distribution. Suitable for theoretical exercises but not realistic.

Case 2: Constant heat loss

Assumes heat loss is constant regardless of position. The temperature does not reach a uniform value.

Case 3: Position-dependent heat loss

Heat loss changes according to surface temperature. This is the most realistic case and qualitatively agrees with the temperature distribution previously shown in NHK footage, where the value became nearly constant after a certain position.

In all cases, the theoretical solution (dashed line) and the numerical result (red line) matched perfectly, confirming the validity of the method.

2. Extension to the two-dimensional steady heat conduction equation

Overview of two-dimensional heat conduction

The one-dimensional concept is extended to heat conduction in a more realistic two-dimensional square plate.

Temperature is expressed as a function of position, T(x, y), and because the state is steady, time t does not appear.

The goal is to discretize the two-dimensional steady heat conduction equation by FVM and perform numerical simulation.

Governing equation

The general governing equation of heat conduction consists of a heat-storage term, a heat-conduction term (Laplacian), and a heat source/sink term.

ρCp * ∂T/∂t = λ * (∂²T/∂x² + ∂²T/∂y²) + Q

α = λ / (ρCp) is the thermal diffusivity.

In steady state, since ∂T/∂t = 0,

∂²T/∂x² + ∂²T/∂y² = -Q / λ

This form is known as the Poisson equation. Because it appears not only in heat conduction but in many areas of physics, learning how to solve it is important. When Q = 0, it becomes the Laplace equation.

Three cases of the two-dimensional simulation

Case 1: Fixed-temperature condition (Dirichlet condition)

The top side is fixed at high temperature TH, and the other three sides are fixed at low temperature TL. Heat is expected to conduct from the top side to the other sides. Heat loss Q is assumed uniform.

Case 2: Another fixed-temperature condition (Dirichlet condition)

The left and right sides are fixed at high temperature TH, and the top and bottom sides are fixed at low temperature TL. Heat is expected to conduct inward from the left and right sides. Q is assumed uniform.

Case 3: Heat loss by convection (Neumann condition)

Convective heat loss is considered on all four sides. The surrounding air temperature is assumed to be TH.

The boundary condition is given by balancing the surface heat flux (Fourier’s law) with the convective heat loss (Newton’s law of cooling, Q = h * (T_surface - T_air)). In this case, the boundary values themselves are also unknowns to be solved. A boundary condition defined by a gradient is called a Neumann condition.

Discretization of the two-dimensional domain

As the first step in applying FVM, the two-dimensional domain is divided into a grid and control volumes and nodes are defined.

The x direction is divided into n elements, and the y direction is also divided into n elements.

The position of each element is represented by (i, j), using i as the x-direction index and j as the y-direction index.

Temperature T is defined at the node center and written as Tij.

The x-direction control-volume length Δxi and the y-direction length Δyj are defined.

This grid is then used to proceed with discretization of the governing equation.

Knowledge Points

1. Derivation of 2D Discretized Equations using FVM

Governing Equation and Control Volume Setup

Begins with the general 2D governing equation containing partial derivatives in x and y.

Applies FVM by integrating the governing equation over a control volume.

Shows a control volume centered at node (i, j) with dimensions Δxᵢ and Δyⱼ.

Defines neighboring nodes: (i-1, j) West, (i+1, j) East, (i, j+1) North, (i, j-1) South.

Labels control volume faces: West (W), East (E), North (N), South (S).

Applying Gauss’s Theorem and Discretizing Terms

Uses Gauss’s theorem to convert second-order derivatives into first-order fluxes at control volume faces.

X-Direction Term: The integral of the second derivative in x becomes the difference of slopes at East and West faces, times cell height Δyⱼ.

East face slope ≈ (Tᵢ₊₁,ⱼ - Tᵢ,ⱼ) / (0.5Δxᵢ₊₁ + 0.5Δxᵢ).

West face slope ≈ (Tᵢ,ⱼ - Tᵢ₋₁,ⱼ) / (0.5Δxᵢ + 0.5Δxᵢ₋₁).

Y-Direction Term: Similarly, the integral in y becomes the difference of slopes at North and South faces, times cell width Δxᵢ.

North face slope ≈ (Tᵢ,ⱼ₊₁ - Tᵢ,ⱼ) / (0.5Δyⱼ₊₁ + 0.5Δyⱼ).

South face slope ≈ (Tᵢ,ⱼ - Tᵢ,ⱼ₋₁) / (0.5Δyⱼ + 0.5Δyⱼ₋₁).

Source Term: The integral of constant source q over the volume is qΔxᵢΔyⱼ.

Formulating the Final Algebraic Equation

Combines discretized terms into one algebraic equation. To keep a systematic structure, flips West and South terms’ order to make signs positive.

Defines neighbor coefficients: Cₑ (East), Cw (West), Cₙ (North), Cs (South).

Cₑ = Δyⱼ / (0.5Δxᵢ + 0.5Δxᵢ₊₁)

Cw = Δyⱼ / (0.5Δxᵢ₋₁ + 0.5Δxᵢ)

Cₙ = Δxᵢ / (0.5Δyⱼ₊₁ + 0.5Δyⱼ)

Cs = Δxᵢ / (0.5Δyⱼ + 0.5Δyⱼ₋₁)

Simplified equation: Cₑ(Tᵢ₊₁,ⱼ - Tᵢ,ⱼ) + Cw(Tᵢ₋₁,ⱼ - Tᵢ,ⱼ) + Cₙ(Tᵢ,ⱼ₊₁ - Tᵢ,ⱼ) + Cs(Tᵢ,ⱼ₋₁ - Tᵢ,ⱼ) = -qΔxᵢΔyⱼ/λ.

Central coefficient C₀ = Cₑ + Cw + Cₙ + Cs.

Rearranged to solve for Tᵢ,ⱼ:

Tᵢ,ⱼ = (1/C₀)[CₑTᵢ₊₁,ⱼ + CwTᵢ₋₁,ⱼ + CₙTᵢ,ⱼ₊₁ + CsTᵢ,ⱼ₋₁] - (qΔxᵢΔyⱼ)/(λC₀).

Shows Tᵢ,ⱼ is a weighted average of neighbor temperatures, with coefficients representing each neighbor’s influence.

2. Generalization and Iterative Solution Methods

Generalization to 1D, 2D, and 3D

The systematic derivation extends across dimensions.

1D Case: Tᵢ depends only on East (i+1) and West (i-1): Tᵢ = (1/C₀)[CₑTᵢ₊₁ + CwTᵢ₋₁].

2D Case: Uses East, West, North (j+1), South (j-1), as derived.

3D Case: Adds Right (R, k+1) and Left (L, k-1). C₀ sums six neighbors (Cₑ + Cw + Cₙ + Cs + Cᵣ + Cₗ). Coefficient formulas extend accordingly; for example, Cᵣ includes ΔxᵢΔyⱼ in the numerator.

Iterative Solution Methods

Jacobi Method: Uses previous iteration (prediction values, superscript P) for all RHS unknowns. Simple but can be slow.

Gauss-Seidel Method: Improves Jacobi by using most recent updates in the current iteration. For loops over i then j, West (i-1) and South (j-1) use updated values; North (j+1) and East (i+1) use prediction values.

Successive Over-Relaxation (SOR): Often paired with Gauss-Seidel to accelerate or stabilize convergence. Computes a temporary Tᵢ,ⱼ* and then updates Tᵢ,ⱼ as a weighted average of Tᵢ,ⱼ* and the previous value, with relaxation coefficient β (0–1).
