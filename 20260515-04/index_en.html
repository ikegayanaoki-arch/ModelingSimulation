Summary

This lecture provides a comprehensive review of the 2D steady-state heat conduction equation using the Finite Volume Method (FVM), followed by an in-depth introduction to the numerical simulation of the 1D unsteady heat conduction equation. The main focus is on tracking the temporal development of temperature distribution in a 1D metal bar. The lecture introduces and contrasts two fundamental numerical methods for solving unsteady problems: the explicit method and the implicit method. It details the discretization of the governing equation, including the new storage term, and explains how the choice of time step for spatial difference terms leads to either the explicit or implicit formulation. The lecture concludes by tasking students with creating flowcharts for both methods to solidify their understanding.

Detailed Summary

Knowledge Points 1. Review of 2D Steady-State Heat Conduction Governing Equation The lecture revisits the steady-state 2D heat conduction equation previously solved: ∂²t/∂x² + ∂²t/∂y² = S , where t is temperature and S is a source term. A typo in the slide ( t instead of ∂²t ) was noted. Finite Volume Method (FVM) Discretization The process involves defining a grid where a point is denoted by indices (i, j). The temperature at this point is T(i,j) . The governing equation is integrated over a control volume. Using Gauss's theorem, the second-order partial derivatives are reduced to first-order derivatives evaluated at the control volume faces (e.g., east, west, north, south). The final discretized equation for the temperature at a node T(i,j) is expressed as a function of its neighboring nodes (T(i+1,j), T(i-1,j), T(i,j+1), T(i,j-1)) and a source term, with coefficients CO, CE, CW, CN, CS representing the influence of each node. Previous Case Studies A brief review of three conditions previously simulated: Case 1: One side heated (top), with the other three sides at a low temperature. Case 2: Two sides heated (left and right), with the top and bottom at a low temperature. Case 3: A heat convection condition where the surrounding air temperature is constant, and boundary temperatures are calculated. The resulting temperature distributions for these cases were shown, illustrating heat transfer from high to low-temperature regions. 2. 1D Unsteady Heat Conduction Simulation Problem Definition The lecture shifts focus from steady-state to unsteady (time-dependent) problems. The simulation is simplified to one dimension. The goal is to track how temperature T changes with both position x and time t. Temperature is a function T(x, t). Governing Equation The 1D unsteady heat conduction equation includes a storage term to account for the change in temperature over time. The equation is: ρ * Cp * (∂T/∂t) = λ * (∂²T/∂x²). ρ * Cp * (∂T/∂t) is the storage term. λ * (∂²T/∂x²) is the diffusion term. ρ (rho) is density, Cp is specific heat, and λ (lambda) is heat conductivity. Boundary and Initial Conditions Boundary Conditions (BC): The temperatures at both ends of the 1D bar are fixed. One end is at a low temperature TL ( T(x=0, t) = TL ), and the other is at a high temperature TH ( T(x=L, t) = TH ). Initial Condition (IC): An initial temperature distribution must be prescribed. For this problem, the initial temperature at time t=0 is uniform and equal to the low temperature TL for all x (T(x, t=0) = TL). Expected Temperature Development The simulation will track the temperature profile as it evolves from the flat initial condition ( t=0 ) towards the linear steady-state solution ( t → ∞ ). At intermediate times, the temperature profile will show a gradual change, respecting the fixed boundary conditions. 3. Discretization of the 1D Unsteady Equation Grid and Node Definition The 1D bar is divided into N control volumes. Nodes are defined at the center of each control volume, indexed by i. Temperature at node i is Ti. A new discretization parameter is introduced: the time step, Δt. Integrating the Governing Equation Using FVM, the governing equation is integrated over the control volume Δx . Diffusion Term (Right-Hand Side): Discretization is identical to the steady-state case, resulting in terms involving temperature differences with neighboring nodes (Ti+1, Ti, Ti-1). Storage Term (Left-Hand Side): The time derivative ∂T/∂t is discretized using a first-order forward difference: (T(n+1) - T(n)) / Δt , where n is the time step index. After integrating over the control volume Δxi, the discretized storage term becomes ρ * Cp * ( (Ti(n+1) - Ti(n)) / Δt ) * Δxi. General Discretized Equation Combining the terms gives the general equation: CO * (Ti(n+1) - Ti(n)) = CE * (T(i+1) - Ti) + CW * (T(i-1) - Ti) . The coefficients are defined as: CO = ρ * Cp / Δt (Note: The instructor's formula in the transcript appears to have missed Δxi , which is part of the integrated volume). CE = λ / (0.5 * (Δx(i+1) + Δxi)). CW = λ / (0.5 * (Δxi + Δx(i-1))). 4. Explicit vs. Implicit Methods Core Concept The key difference lies in the time step ( n or n+1 ) at which the spatial difference terms (diffusion term) are evaluated. Explicit Method Definition: The spatial gradients are evaluated at the previous time step, n . Equation: The temperature at the next time step Ti(n+1) is calculated explicitly using only known values from the previous time step n. Ti(n+1) = (1/CO) * [ CE*Ti+1(n) + CW*Ti-1(n) + (CO - CE - CW)*Ti(n) ] Characteristics: Computationally efficient per time step as it's a direct calculation. Does not require an iterative solver. The next state is determined in a single step from the previous state. As visualized, the value at (i, n+1) depends on the values at (i-1, n), (i, n), and (i+1, n). Implicit Method Definition: The spatial gradients are evaluated at the current (unknown) time step, n+1 . Equation: The equation for Ti(n+1) now involves unknown temperatures of its neighbors at the same n+1 time step (Ti+1(n+1) and Ti-1(n+1)). Ti(n+1) = (1/CD) * [ CE*Ti+1(n+1) + CW*Ti-1(n+1) + CO*Ti(n) ] , where CD = CO + CE + CW . Characteristics: This forms a system of simultaneous linear equations for all nodes at time n+1 . An iterative method (like Gauss-Seidel, used in steady-state problems) is required to solve for the temperatures at n+1 because Ti(n+1) depends on Ti+1(n+1) and Ti-1(n+1), which are also unknown. As visualized, the value at (i, n+1) depends on the value at (i, n) and the unknown values at (i-1, n+1) and (i+1, n+1). Announcements The lecture on May 22, 2026, is canceled. Summary

This lecture explains the differences between explicit and implicit methods for solving one-dimensional unsteady heat conduction problems. The instructor illustrates the procedural differences using flowcharts, emphasizing that the explicit method is a simple one-loop process, while the implicit method introduces an inner iteration loop to ensure convergence at each time step. A central theme is the theoretical analysis of numerical stability via convergence criteria. The lecture derives the diffusion number (R) and shows that the explicit method is "conditionally stable," requiring R < 0.5, which tightly constrains the time step (Δt) relative to the spatial step (Δx). In contrast, the implicit method is "unconditionally stable," converging regardless of time step size. The lecture also clarifies that numerical stability does not guarantee accuracy and concludes with a programming exercise for implementing both methods in Python.

Detailed Summary

Knowledge Points 1. Comparison of Explicit and Implicit Methods Fundamental Procedural Difference The explicit method computes the temperature at the next time step (n+1) directly from the temperatures at the current time step (n). It does not require iteration to achieve convergence at each time step. The implicit method computes the temperature at the next time step (n+1) based on temperatures at that same future time step (n+1), necessitating an iterative process at each time step to converge before proceeding. Flowchart and Loop Structure Explicit Method: Uses a simple, single-loop process: calculate terms, update values to the next time step, and repeat this time loop until reaching steady state or a predefined end time. Implicit Method: Employs nested loops. An outer time loop advances time, while an inner iteration (convergence) loop at each time step repeatedly updates temperatures until the solution converges (i.e., the change between iterations is sufficiently small). 2. Numerical Stability and Convergence Criteria Concept of Convergence Criteria Determines whether a numerical method yields a stable, converged solution or diverges. The analysis examines the coefficient multiplying the previous time step temperature (Tᵢⁿ) in the geometric series formed by repeated time-step updates. Explicit Method: Conditional Stability Derivation: The next-step temperature (Tᵢⁿ⁺¹) is proportional to (1 - 2R) * Tᵢⁿ , where R is the diffusion number. Diffusion Number (R): A key dimensionless parameter, R = A * Δt / (Δx)², with A the thermal diffusivity (λ / (ρ * c_p)), Δt the time step, and Δx the grid spacing. Stability Condition: For convergence, (1 - 2R) must lie between -1 and 1, yielding 0 < R < 0.5. Practical Implication: Time step constraint: Δt < (Δx)² / (2A). Halving Δx forces Δt to be reduced by a factor of four, increasing computation time. Name: "Conditionally stable," as stability depends on specific Δt and Δx choices. Implicit Method: Unconditional Stability Derivation: The next-step temperature (Tᵢⁿ⁺¹) is proportional to (1 / (1 + 2R)) * Tᵢⁿ . Stability Condition: 1 / (1 + 2R) lies between 0 and 1 for any positive R (since A, Δt, and Δx are positive). Practical Implication: Numerically stable for any Δt or Δx. Name: "Unconditionally stable." Its robustness makes it common in commercial simulation software, allowing users to set parameters without deep numerical expertise and still obtain stable solutions. Stability vs. Accuracy Numerical stability and accuracy are distinct. An unconditionally stable implicit method avoids divergence but may still be inaccurate. Accuracy for both methods requires sufficiently small Δt and Δx to reduce discretization error and closely approximate the continuous solution. Even the implicit method needs fine Δt for high accuracy. Questions [Insert Question/Confusion] Assignments 1.
